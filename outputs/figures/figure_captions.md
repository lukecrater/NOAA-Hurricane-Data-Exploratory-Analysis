# Figure Descriptions

## Figure 1: Atlantic Storms per Year, 1851–2025 (`01_storms_per_year.png`)

This figure plots the number of Atlantic tropical cyclones tracked each year from 1851 to 2025, split into all named/tracked storms and the subset that reached hurricane strength (Category 1 or higher), with a 10-year rolling average overlaid to smooth out year-to-year noise. Two reference lines mark 1944, when routine aircraft reconnaissance began, and 1966, the start of the satellite era — both moments after which the storm count rises noticeably. This isn't necessarily evidence of more storms forming; it's at least partly an artifact of better detection, since storms that stayed out at sea and were never observed by ship traffic simply weren't recorded in the pre-aircraft, pre-satellite era. Any claim about a long-term trend in storm frequency has to account for this detection bias first.

## Figure 2: Atlantic Basin Track Density by Category, 1851–2025 (`02_track_density_map.png`)

This is a scatter plot of every recorded storm position in the HURDAT2 database, color-coded by Saffir-Simpson category at that point in time, spanning the full Atlantic basin. It reveals the basin's structural patterns at a glance: the dense cluster of storm genesis and early tracks off the African coast and moving through the Caribbean and Gulf of Mexico, the characteristic northward recurvature as storms are steered by the subtropical ridge, and the concentration of the strongest storms (orange/red/purple) in the warm waters of the Caribbean, Gulf, and western Atlantic rather than the open ocean. Sanibel Island is marked with a black star for geographic reference, sitting near the western edge of the most storm-dense region of the basin.

## Figure 3: Peak Intensity and Rapid Intensification Distributions (`03_intensity_and_ri.png`)

This two-panel figure characterizes how strong storms typically get and how quickly. The left panel is a histogram of each storm's peak sustained wind speed, showing that most Atlantic storms top out well below hurricane strength, with a long tail of major hurricanes. The right panel shows the distribution of each storm's largest wind-speed increase within any trailing 24-hour window, with a reference line at 35 knots — the National Hurricane Center's official threshold for "rapid intensification." Only about 9% of storms on record ever cross that threshold, which frames how unusual Hurricane Ian's own intensification was: Ian gained 110 knots in the 24 hours before its Florida landfall, placing it far into the tail of this distribution.

## Figure 4: Hurricane Ian vs. Historical SW Florida Storm Tracks (`04_ian_vs_sw_florida_tracks.png`)

This figure overlays Hurricane Ian's full track (in red) against the tracks of all 125 storms on record that have passed within roughly 90 nautical miles of Sanibel Island since 1851 (in gray), with Sanibel marked by a star. It provides geographic context for how Ian fits among the storms that have historically threatened the region — most regional storms track through the Gulf or up the Florida peninsula from the south, and Ian's path, curving north out of the Caribbean before turning east into Southwest Florida, sits within that established pattern rather than being a geographic outlier. What made Ian exceptional wasn't its path, but its intensity when it got there.

## Figure 5: Hurricane Ian's Intensity Over Time (`05_ian_intensity_curve.png`)

This chart tracks Hurricane Ian's maximum sustained wind speed from its formation in the Caribbean through its dissipation, with the rapid-intensification window (the period where it gained at least 35 knots in 24 hours) shaded in orange and each of its five landfalls marked with vertical lines. The curve shows Ian intensifying sharply in the two days before reaching Southwest Florida, peaking at 140 knots — Category 5 strength by post-season reanalysis — just before landfall near Cayo Costa. This is the clearest single visualization of why Ian was so destructive: the storm strengthened dramatically in the final approach, leaving little time for forecasts (or residents) to fully adjust to its true intensity.

## Figure 6: Track and Intensity Error — Our Model vs. NHC Official Forecast (`06_ian_model_vs_nhc.png`)

This two-panel bar chart compares the forecasting model built for this project against the National Hurricane Center's own published forecast accuracy, at 24, 48, and 72-hour lead times. The model's error is calculated by applying it to Hurricane Ian's real track — which it never saw during training — at every observation point, the same way NHC verifies its own forecasts. The NHC bars are the agency's official published average error across all Atlantic storms in the 2022 season, not Ian-specific. The comparison is intentionally not close: the project's model, which relies only on a storm's own recent position, speed, and intensity trend, produces roughly 3x the track error and 2–3x the intensity error of NHC's operational forecasts. That gap is itself the finding — it quantifies how much of NHC's forecasting skill comes from atmospheric model guidance and human forecaster judgment, rather than from patterns in a storm's own recent behavior alone.

## Figure 7: Hurricane Ian — Predicted vs. Actual Position by Lead Time (`07_ian_predicted_tracks.png`)

This three-panel figure visualizes the model's individual predictions against Ian's actual track (in black), with one panel each for 24, 48, and 72-hour forecasts. Each colored line connects a point on Ian's real track to where the model predicted the storm would be that many hours later; short, tightly clustered lines indicate accurate forecasts, while long, divergent lines indicate large errors. The spread widens substantially from the 24-hour to the 72-hour panel, and the largest errors cluster around Ian's northward recurvature in the Gulf of Mexico — the point where the storm's path changed most sharply, and precisely the kind of turn that a model using only recent motion, rather than atmospheric steering patterns, struggles to anticipate.

## Figure 8: Why Sanibel Took the Hit It Did (`08_ian_impact_layer.png`)

This two-panel figure connects Hurricane Ian's meteorological data to its real-world impact on Sanibel Island and Fort Myers Beach. The left panel shows Ian's final approach into Southwest Florida, with the track colored by wind speed, illustrating how the storm was already at or near its peak intensity as it crossed the coast. The right panel presents storm-surge inundation heights surveyed by NOAA and USGS after the storm — up to 15 feet above ground level at Fort Myers Beach and 9 to 13 feet across Sanibel Island — measured against a reference line for typical barrier-island ground elevation of just 0 to 3 feet above sea level. That gap between surge height and island elevation explains why the flooding was so catastrophic, and why storm surge was Ian's deadliest hazard nationally, responsible for 41 deaths, 36 of them in Lee County alone. All figures in this panel are drawn from NHC's official Tropical Cyclone Report for Ian, not modeled or estimated.
